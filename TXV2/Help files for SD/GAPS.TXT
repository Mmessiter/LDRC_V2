How long the radio link took to come back each time it was lost,|
sorted by length: a quick way to judge the link.|
Look at it after landing, before you switch off. Not while flying!||
READING IT:|
Each column is one range of times in milliseconds (ms), written|
under it: '< 4' means under 4 ms.|
The number under a column: how many reconnections took that|
long. Its coloured bar grows with it, full at 50 or more.|
The per cent above a column: its share of all reconnections.|
Along the bottom: the 'Shortest', 'Average' and 'Longest' times.||
Green columns (under 100 ms) are normal. Amber (100 to 249 ms)|
and red (250 ms or more) are long gaps.||
The longest time may have happened only once or twice, while most|
were far shorter. This can be reassuring news!||
WHAT IS NORMAL:|
Reconnections are usually extremely short with this system:|
below 8 ms, and sometimes below 4 ms.|
You only notice gaps above about 350 ms (a third of a second).|
Many above 100 ms, or even 250 ms? Then consider moving|
the aerials, and check those on the receiver and on the|
transmitter for damage.||
WHY THERE ARE GAPS:|
Many models hop between frequencies (FHSS) at the same time.|
Collisions cannot be avoided, but they are brief: the system|
reconnects as fast as it can.||
NOT COUNTED:|
- gaps that begin while the safety is on, with a Rotorflight|
model that has an arming channel;|
- gaps while a V2 receiver is saving flight data.|
The log still notes these two (with 'Keep log files' on).|
Gaps while the 'PID (Rotorflight)' page is open are|
neither counted nor logged.||
'Clear', on the Telemetry page, sets the counts back to zero.|
They also start again a few seconds after each connection, and|
when you press 'OK' on the 'Rotorflight' page.|
